Sweet-Haus

client · 2026

A custom cabin configurator and a unified marketing site for Sweet-Haus, a tiny-home and cabin builder in Chesnee, South Carolina.

what they said

“Buying a home shouldn’t take weeks of back-and-forth just to learn what it costs … We believe this is the most in-depth design tool in the tiny home industry, and the first to take a customer from choosing colors to paying a deposit and getting a place in production.”

Todd Garland, CEO of Sweet-Haus, in the company’s November 2026 press release

the ask

Sweet-Haus wanted customers to design and price a cabin online, with every request reaching the sales team as a structured lead. Their marketing site also ran on another development company’s CMS, so they wanted it brought in-house.

what i built

  • A configurator that, at launch, swapped between 181 real renders across 7 models as customers chose paint, roof and trim, falling back to the closest render when a combination didn’t exist.
  • A pricing engine covering base price, options and add-ons, with a monthly payment calculator and a shipping estimate based on distance.
  • A submit pipeline that validates each build, saves it and hands it to the sales team in under a second.
  • An admin area for models, options, pricing and submissions, with CSV export.
  • The first version of the unified site: 33 pages migrated from the old site, with 301 redirects, a sitemap and search metadata.

how it works

One Next.js app serves the marketing pages and the configurator. Models, options and prices live in Supabase and are edited in the admin area; the site caches the catalog for five minutes and falls back to a bundled copy if the database can’t be reached.

A design partner refined the site’s look before launch, and Sweet-Haus’s own team has added features since.

the submit pipeline

When a customer submits a build, the server re-checks the build and recalculates its prices instead of trusting the browser, and a hidden field screens out bots. The build is saved to Supabase first, then handed to the proposal portal, which emails the customer a link to their build and notifies the team, then synced to Airtable and recorded as a conversion. The team and the customer are typically notified in under a second.

hard problems

  • Renders that didn’t match the color buttons. The client’s render filenames were inconsistent, and colors had been renamed. I made the files on disk the source of truth: a build step writes a manifest of every render, and a resolver matches each choice through color aliases, falling back to the nearest render that exists.
  • Moving DNS off the old agency without breaking email. I inventoried every record, recreated them ahead of time on the new host, then switched nameservers, keeping the old setup as a rollback. A stale DNSSEC record left behind made validating resolvers reject the domain; I found and removed it. Email never went down.
  • A silent failure inherited from the old site. Pre-launch checks with real test submissions found that the old site’s lead notifications had been failing quietly for weeks because a subdomain had no DNS record. I restored it and verified every notification path end to end before cutover.
  • Keeping search traffic. Redirects came from a copy of the old site and an export of the old CMS’s database. Before launch I checked every page and redirect pattern, and 25 live checks after launch passed with zero failures.

working with their team

I set up a sandbox branch with its own preview deployment and wrote setup guides, so Sweet-Haus’s team and their design partner can make and ship changes with Claude Code. They have shipped features through pull requests since launch.

stack

Next.js, React, TypeScript and Tailwind. Supabase (Postgres, Storage and an Edge Function). Resend and Airtable. Vercel and Vercel DNS.
